# The four structures

Beat structures taken from native ads that performed. The structures are the
reusable asset — build every beat from your own product, category, and research.

Each one exists to do the same job in a different voice: get a cold stranger from
a scroll to a click by making them feel recognised, then reframed, then hopeful.

---

## Structure 1 — The Insider Confession

**Voice:** someone who works in the category. **CTA:** direct to product.

**The only requirement:** a person who sees the pattern all day and can admit
they were wrong about it. Confession is what buys the trust, because advertising
does not concede.

### Who the insider can be

| Insider | Credential beat | Their subject | Their "result" |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Stylist / colourist** | Years behind the chair, heads of hair, tools tested | A client who has sat in her chair for years | What she can see in the mirror, the state of the ends, six months apart |
| **Engineer / product developer** | Years designing or manufacturing in the category | Not a person — a spec, a product line, a factory | A material property, a tolerance, a teardown |
| **Salon owner** | Years staffing and buying tools | A stylist on the team, or a repeat client | What the salon stopped replacing |
| **Retailer / buyer** | Years choosing what goes on the shelf | What sells and what comes back | Return rates, what customers say at the counter |

**Pick the authority the reader already trusts in this category.** A stylist
outranks a doctor on hair. A mechanic outranks an engineer on what breaks. An
engineer outranks both on why it was built that way. Borrowed prestige from the
wrong field reads as a stretch and costs more trust than it buys.

### Beats

1. **Confession opener.** Credential plus an admission against interest, loop
   left open.
   > "I've been styling hair for 19 years, and I've been telling clients the
   > wrong thing about heat."
2. **Tenure and the pattern.** How long, and what always happens. Fragments:
   *"Thinner. Always thinner. A little less at the crown every year."*
3. **The break.** *"Last month, it didn't."*
4. **The subject.** A specific client, a specific length of relationship, a
   specific recurring moment. Named tools she owns, with prices.
5. **The disbelief.** The insider does not believe it. This is what makes the
   reader believe it.
6. **The outside source.** The breakthrough came from someone with no authority
   at all — a client, a friend, a comment thread. **Never from the insider.**
   This is the load-bearing beat: it is what stops the piece reading as a pitch.
7. **The failed attempts, itemised.** Named products, named prices, what each did
   and did not do.
8. **The mechanism reframe.** Why everything they tried could not have worked,
   **while defending those attempts.** Not "those tools are junk" — "those tools
   were never designed for what your hair became." Nobody who feels stupid buys
   anything.
9. **The insider validates it.** They already half knew the underlying principle;
   they had never connected it. Converts the mechanism from a claim into a
   confirmation.
10. **The specification gate.** The mechanism only works if a specific thing is
    true — the material, the tolerance, the way it is made. This is the beat that
    does the selling, because it is what makes one product the answer rather than
    the category.
11. **The transformation timeline.** Week one, week three, month two. Milestones
    are **behavioural, not technical** — and the last one is an image, not a
    metric.
12. **The qualification block.** A run of "if you" lines letting the reader
    self-select.
13. **The absolution.** *"You're not doing it wrong."* Placed immediately before
    the offer, so the CTA lands after a moment of being believed.
14. **Offer.** Price framing, availability, guarantee with a specific window,
    link.
15. **The wish.** *"You'll wish you'd found this years ago."*

### The engineer variant

Reorders slightly and is **the strongest mechanism vehicle of any structure
here.** Beats 4 and 5 are replaced by a **teardown**: what is actually inside the
thing they already own, what it costs to make, and why it fails in a specific way
the reader has definitely experienced. Beat 8 onward runs unchanged.

> "I design heated styling tools. Here's what we don't put on the box."

It needs no client story, no disbelief scene, and no borrowed emotion — the
authority is technical rather than personal, which makes it the cleanest of all
the variants to write and the hardest to write badly.

## Structure 2 — The Sufferer Confession

**Voice:** first person, the person with the problem. **CTA:** an article or
prelander, not the product.

**Best for:** anything with shame attached, an audience burned by expert advice,
and a soft first click.

### Beats

1. **Rank the pain, put shame on top.** Naming the real injury before the
   symptoms *is* the opener.
   > "It wasn't the frizz. It wasn't even the breakage. The worst part was the
   > photographs."
2. **Shame in micro moments.** Not adjectives — specific withdrawals. What she
   stopped doing. Who she stopped standing next to. The fifteen minutes she
   spends somewhere private, every day.
3. **Duration in lived units.** *"Two summers of the same routine."* Seasons and
   events land harder than months.
4. **The routine that failed**, as a fragment list. Each line one product or one
   step.
5. **The same result.** *"Brief improvement. Then back."*
6. **The expert's useless advice, each one answered.** *"She told me to cut it
   shorter. I'd already cut it twice."* This is the trust engine — it proves she
   did everything right.
7. **The late-night research.** What she had to find out alone, at 1am, because
   nobody explained it.
8. **The withheld reason.** *"There's a specific reason. And once you understand
   it, three years of failed products suddenly make complete sense."* The
   mechanism is **promised, never delivered.** That withholding is what the click
   buys.
9. **The handoff.** Someone qualified has written the full breakdown.
10. **The negative definition.** *"It's not another list of products to try."*
    Pre-empts what the reader assumes is behind the click.
11. **Soft CTA.** Read the article.

## Structure 3 — The Symptom Checklist

**Voice:** direct second person. **CTA:** a named system or product.

**Best for:** a cluster of things the reader has not connected, a contrarian
reframe, and an audience that does not yet know they have a definable problem.
**The only structure that works on people who have not tried anything yet.**

### Beats

1. **The effort paradox.** What they are doing right, and the outcome that
   contradicts it. *"You're using the heat protectant. You're air drying twice a
   week. And it's still breaking."*
2. **The unconnected list.** Second person, each line a separate recognition —
   things they have never linked to one cause.
3. **The one that lands hardest**, called out as such: *"and here's the one that
   really messes with your head."* Usually a social comparison.
4. **The check.** *"Am I right?"*
5. **The reversal.** The contrarian claim, stated flatly.
6. **Acknowledge the resistance.** *"That probably just annoyed you."* Naming the
   objection defuses it.
7. **The cited mechanism.** A named source and a specific effect, then the chain
   in plain language, then why the thing they have been doing caused it.
8. **The named method.** The mechanism becomes a branded thing.
9. **Numbers moving counter-intuitively.**
10. **What's included.** A concrete list.
11. **Price, then guarantee**, with friction removed.
12. **P.S.** Reframes the authority who failed them.

## Structure 4 — The Soft Handoff

**Voice:** insider. **CTA:** an article or quiz.

A hybrid — Structure 1's credibility with Structure 2's withheld payoff. The
insider explains why the whole category fails, then hands off rather than
selling.

**Best for:** a considered or expensive purchase, or a cold audience that will
not buy on a first click. Lowest friction of the four, and it needs the strongest
page behind it because the ad has done none of the selling.

### Beats

1. Credential and the pattern they see daily
2. The category-wide failure, stated plainly and against interest
3. The structural reason nobody explains it — not malice, just how the business
   works
4. The mechanism named but not explained
5. What the linked piece is, defined by what it is not
6. Soft CTA

## Choosing

| | Product-direct CTA | Article / prelander CTA |
|---|---|---|
| **Insider voice** | 1 — Insider Confession | 4 — Soft Handoff |
| **Sufferer voice** | 3 — Symptom Checklist | 2 — Sufferer Confession |

Three further cuts:

**Is there shame in this problem?** If yes, Structure 2 outperforms — shame is
the one emotion an authority voice cannot speak to credibly.

**Have they tried and failed at specific named things?** If yes, Structures 1 and
2 both work and the itemised failure list is your strongest section. If they have
tried nothing, Structure 3 is the only fit.

**Is the mechanism physical?** If the product wins on how it is built, the
engineer variant of Structure 1 beats everything else here.

---

## Checks before delivering

- Does beat one concede something, or does it start selling?
- Does the breakthrough arrive from **outside** the authority?
- Does the mechanism **defend** what failed them rather than blaming them?
- Is every claim a specific number, name, price, or spec?
- Is the transformation a sequence of behavioural moments, not adjectives?
- Does the CTA follow a moment of being believed?
- **Zero hyphens?**
- Read aloud: does the rhythm break into fragments, or run flat?
- Could a reader tell this was an ad before the CTA?
- Is every credential something the page can stand behind?
