# Eleven winning image ads

World-class ads used as the reference set for headline writing. Each headline is
given with its structural move and how the image carries it.

Use them as **patterns to rebuild**, never as templates to fill. When a headline
cites one of these, it should be citing the *move*, not the wording.

---

## 1. Ryanair — "The world's cheapest airline."

**Move:** Absurd price equivalence.

Bold solid blue background, symmetrical layout, two open boxes side by side
separated by "vs." Left: an ordinary pizza, labelled **PIZZA $19.99**. Right: an
identical box containing a grayscale Leaning Tower of Pisa, fitted like a slice,
labelled **LONDON → PISA $19.99**, with a small plane graphic between the
cities. Tagline below, "cheapest" highlighted in yellow.

**Why it works:** It doesn't claim cheapness, it demonstrates it against
something the viewer already prices instinctively. The pizza/Pisa pun makes it
memorable; the equivalence makes it persuasive.

---

## 2. Tesla — "It takes 3.1 seconds to read this ad."

**Move:** Self-referential proof.

Clean white background. Bold black headline, then smaller gray subhead: *"The
same time it takes a Model S to go from 0 to 60 mph."* A black Model S in side
profile, rear motion-blurred. Tesla logo at the bottom.

**Why it works:** The reader experiences the claim while reading it. The proof
is the act of reading, so there's nothing to disbelieve.

---

## 3. Nutella — "This is bread. This is breakfast."

**Move:** Redefinition through minimal contrast.

White background, symmetrical. Left: plain toasted bread, captioned *"This is
bread."* Right: identical toast with Nutella spread in a heart, captioned
*"This is breakfast."* Tagline: "Turning bread into breakfast since 1964."

**Why it works:** One variable changes and the category changes with it. The
product isn't described at all — it's the thing that did the transformation.
This is the anti-categorisation move in visual form.

---

## 4. Patrón — "You miss 100% of the shots you don't take."

**Move:** Double entendre on a phrase already in the culture.

White background, bold black serif headline. Centre: Patrón Silver bottle,
cork stopper, green and yellow ribbon, a shot glass with a single ice cube, lime
wedge at the base. Bee logo below.

**Why it works:** Borrows a motivational quote everyone knows and lands it on
the literal product. No claim about the tequila is made at all.

---

## 5. Bible App — "Zero stars. Would not recommend."

**Move:** Inverted negative review.

Subway panel, black background, white text. Headline "Zero stars.", five empty
outlined stars, then *"Would not recommend."* — attributed to **"— Satan"**. App
logo bottom.

**Why it works:** Reads as a damning review until the attribution flips it into
the strongest possible endorsement. The reader completes the joke themselves,
which is what makes it stick.

---

## 6. Chipotle — "Usually, when you roll something this good, it's illegal."

**Move:** Innuendo carried entirely by the product shot.

Pure white background, vertically centred. A foil-wrapped burrito at the top,
headline in the middle, branding at the bottom.

**Why it works:** The joke needs the product to complete it, so the product
can't be ignored.

---

## 7. Corona — "Good things take lime."

**Move:** Single-word pun on a familiar phrase.

**Why it works:** Compresses the brand's entire ritual — the lime in the bottle
— into three words. The pun is the mnemonic.

---

## 8. "Need better way to stay cool?"

**Move:** Direct problem question.

**Why it works:** The plainest pattern in the set, and it still earns its place.
On a problem-aware avatar, naming the problem outperforms cleverness. Reach for
this when the audience already feels the pain and doesn't need convincing it
exists.

---

## 9. "Picks up five times more women than a Lamborghini."

**Move:** Absurd comparison against a status benchmark.

**Why it works:** Specific multiplier plus an unexpected comparator. The number
makes it feel measured; the comparator makes it funny. Note the specificity —
"five times" rather than "more".

---

## 10. "We're Dwayne Johnson's favourite cereal."

**Move:** Borrowed celebrity authority, stated flatly.

**Why it works:** No adjectives, no persuasion. A single verifiable-sounding
fact does all the work. **Only usable when true** — this is exactly the kind of
claim the compliance rule covers.

---

## 11. Huel — "Grab life by the bowls."

**Move:** Pun on an idiom, tied to product form.

Callouts reinforce convenience: *"Ready in 5 minutes."* Huel logo bottom-left in
bold white.

**Why it works:** Tone is bold, empowering, approachable — nutrition without
sacrificing taste or time. The pun carries attitude; the callout carries the
practical objection.

---

## Patterns, extracted

| Pattern | Ads | Use when |
|---|---|---|
| Price/value equivalence | 1 | Value is the angle and the number is startling |
| Self-referential proof | 2 | The claim can be demonstrated in the reading |
| Redefinition by contrast | 3 | The product transforms an ordinary thing |
| Cultural double entendre | 4, 6, 7, 11 | A known phrase lands on the product |
| Inverted review | 5 | Social proof, told against type |
| Direct problem question | 8 | Problem-aware avatar, pain already felt |
| Absurd comparison | 9 | A specific multiplier plus a status benchmark |
| Flat authority claim | 10 | You have a real, verifiable endorsement |

---

## Writing against this set

**Cite the move, not the words.** "Inspired by Nutella — redefinition by
contrast" is useful. Rewriting "This is X. This is Y" for every brand is not.

**Congruency is mandatory.** Every one of these images makes the headline
inevitable. An image that merely coexists with the line has already failed.

**Six to eight words.** Every headline here obeys it. The Tesla one is nine and
earns it by making the length the point.

**Simplicity is not weakness.** #8 is the plainest ad in the set and belongs
here. Cleverness that costs clarity loses to a plain question that names the
avatar's problem.
