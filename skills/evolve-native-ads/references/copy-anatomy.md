# Copy anatomy

The six parts, what each has to accomplish, and how to tell when one has failed.

---

## 1. The hook

One sentence. Two routes, pick one.

**Route A — mirror the image.** The first sentence describes the same moment the
image shows, so precisely that the reader feels it is their own life. Then it
leaves something unresolved. The gap is the point: it becomes psychologically
uncomfortable to stop reading.

**Route B — the blunt truth.** A statement so unexpectedly, bluntly true that
they are stopped before consciously deciding to engage. Often an admission
against interest, or something the category never says out loud.

The strongest openers do both at once — authority plus confession plus an
unclosed loop, inside a dozen words:

> "I've been styling hair for 19 years, and I've been telling clients the wrong
> thing about heat."

The reader cannot leave without knowing what the wrong thing was.

**Failure test:** read only the first sentence. Is there a question in the
reader's mind that only reading on will answer? If not, it is an opening line,
not a hook.

---

## 2. Agitation

Go deeper into the problem in the exact language from the research.

Not "many women struggle with damaged hair." The specific lived detail only
someone who has been through it would recognise:

- The moment in the bathroom
- The thing they said to their partner
- The thing they stopped doing because it got too hard

Use named, dated, priced specifics — the tool that cost £180 and lives in a
drawer, the 3pm meeting, the two summers of the same routine. **Specificity is
what makes it feel reported rather than written.**

**Failure test:** could this paragraph appear in a competitor's ad for a
different product? If yes, it is generic.

---

## 3. Let the fear breathe

The cost of doing nothing. Truthfully, not manipulatively.

This is the shortest section and the one most often skipped. Usually one or two
sentences that sit still instead of rushing to the solution — the quiet
acknowledgement of where this goes if nothing changes.

Keep it restrained. No threats, no statistics, no scare copy. Name something the
reader is **already privately afraid of.** That is the difference between
agitation and fear mongering: you are naming their fear, not manufacturing a new
one.

---

## 4. The aha moment

**The most important structural element in the piece.**

One mechanism. One reframe. Something they have genuinely never heard, explained
so plainly and truthfully that it lands immediately.

It has one job: explain why **everything they already tried could not have
worked** — not because they did something wrong, but because those things were
never designed to address the actual root cause.

This is where hope enters. The reader thinks *oh. That is why.* And then they
want to know what does address it.

The construction that works:

> "The tools aren't bad tools. They were built for hair that behaves the way
> yours used to."

Note what it does. It **defends** the thing that failed them, which removes any
sense of being sold to. It relocates the problem one level deeper. And it gives
the reader something they can repeat to someone else.

Three requirements:

- **Repeatable.** If they cannot explain it to a friend in one sentence, it is
  too complex.
- **Blame removing.** The failure was the design, never the reader.
- **Congruent with the prelander.** Same idea at a different depth, never two
  different ideas.

**Failure test:** does it explain why past attempts failed? A mechanism that only
explains why yours works is a feature, not an aha.

---

## 5. Transformation

Show the other side. Not fantasy — specific, close, believable, one decision
away.

A timeline beats a promise, because it sequences small moments into a life that
feels different. Milestones should be **behavioural, not technical**, and the
last one should be an image rather than a metric:

> she stood in the middle of the group photo instead of behind it

That is a whole transformation in one line. Not "improved confidence" — a
specific thing she did at a specific event.

Mirror the prelander's transformation language so arriving feels continuous.

**Failure test:** does it feel earned by what came before, or dropped in?

---

## 6. The CTA

Not a command. Not a sales line. A gentle, inevitable continuation — what a
friend would say about where to go next.

By this point clicking should feel like the only logical thing left, and not
clicking should feel like the stranger choice.

Earn it with a qualification block first — a run of "if you" lines that let the
reader self-select:

> If you've got a drawer with three tools in it and none of them do what they
> did five years ago. If you've started booking the appointment instead of doing
> it yourself.

Then the release: **"You're not doing it wrong."** The CTA follows a moment of
being believed, which is why it does not feel like a pitch.

Point at whatever the prelander offers — read the article, watch the video, take
the quiz — so the transition feels intentional.

---

## Rhythm

The format's signature is short lines with sudden stops. Fragments used as
sentences. Then a longer passage when something needs explaining, then back to
short.

> Thinner.
>
> Always thinner.
>
> A little less at the crown every year.

Four fragments doing what one flat sentence would have done. Read the copy aloud
— if it moves at one steady pace throughout, the rhythm is not working.

---

## Pre delivery checks

- Does sentence one create a gap that only reading on closes?
- Is the agitation in the customer's words, not cleaned up?
- Does the aha explain why past attempts failed?
- Is the aha the same mechanism as the prelander?
- Is the transformation a sequence of specific moments, not adjectives?
- Does the CTA read like a friend, not a brand?
- **Zero hyphens?**
- Any sentence that sounds like a brochure?
- Read aloud: does the rhythm vary, or is it flat?
- Could a reader tell this was an ad before the CTA? If yes, rewrite.
